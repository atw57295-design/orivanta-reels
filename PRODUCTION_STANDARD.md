# ORIVANTA — Cinematic Documentary Production Standard (permanent)

**Status: ADOPTED, permanent, applies to every ORIVANTA film, episode, trailer, short-form
video and future production**, effective 2026-11-12 per the owner's instruction.

Full text as supplied by the owner:

---

## 1. The core rule: show what the narrator is saying
Every visual must directly support the narration (scientist → show the scientist/accurate
reconstruction; agriculture → fields/crops/machinery; phosphate → mines/processing/trade;
aviation → aircraft/airports; money → financial activity/documents; a named city, company,
event or location → the correct place or a clearly identified representation). Never use
attractive-but-irrelevant footage as filler. Visual accuracy over decorative beauty.

## 2. Cinematic footage — not a slideshow
Prioritize real cinematic moving footage: realistic historical/industrial reconstructions,
natural human activity, multiple camera angles/shot sizes, aerial/tracking/close-up/wide/detail
shots, historically appropriate environments and technology. Stills are permitted when
historically appropriate but must not replace footage through long sequences.
**Never disguise a still image with a slow zoom and present it as genuine video footage.
Never freeze a frame to extend a scene. Never reverse-loop a short clip. Never repeat the
same shot merely to fill the narration.**

## 3. Continuous cinematic storytelling
Every shot has a narrative purpose; progress forward with the story; no repeated
forward/backward movement between the same scenes; no unnecessary returns to previous
footage; no abrupt/random/meaningless transitions; maintain geographic and chronological
logic. The film should feel like one continuous cinematic journey.

## 4. Precision synchronization
Build a full narration-to-visual timeline before editing: exact sentence, word/phrase
timestamps, visual subject, footage/image required, shot start/end time, on-screen text,
subtitle timing, SFX/music cues, and the transition to the next shot. New subjects get their
visual at the right moment — not several seconds late, not before context is established.
Frame-level timing where needed (≈33.3ms/frame at 30fps) — no claiming more precision than
the source audio/timeline actually has.

## 5. Human subjects and historical reconstructions
People must look and move naturally (scientists at lab equipment, workers at machinery,
farmers harvesting, engineers inspecting facilities, pilots flying, historical figures in
historically appropriate settings). Historically accurate clothing/locations/equipment/era.
Avoid artificial faces, unnatural movement, distorted hands, random gestures, inconsistent
character appearance. A specific historical individual must stay visually consistent
throughout a documentary.

## 6. Maps, flags, diagrams, information graphics
Use them when they explain something better than footage: geographic maps, historical
borders, flags, battle locations/movements, trade routes, timelines, equations/industrial
processes, economic charts. Maps must be geographically accurate; labels/dates/numbers
readable and synced to narration. Balance graphics with cinematic footage — not a constant
chart sequence.

## 7. Opening hook — first 3 to 7 seconds
Never a long channel-logo animation to open. Start immediately with a powerful visual, a
surprising verified fact, a compelling question, a dramatic historical moment, or a strong
curiosity-driving statement. Branding stays subtle and never delays the story.

## 8. Narration, subtitles, on-screen text
Natural, confident American English narration, clear pronunciation, professional pacing.
Subtitles must accurately match narration. Highlight key words/numbers exactly when spoken.
Consistent typography, controlled text animation, readable on mobile and TV. No excessive
captions/decoration/unnecessary effects. Never show text that contradicts the narration.

## 9. Sound design and music
Cinematic, subject-appropriate original music; realistic environmental SFX synced to visible
events; narration always clear above music; smooth audio transitions; avoid noticeably
repetitive music loops; avoid excessive dramatic impacts/stingers.

## 10. Visual quality and color grading
Consistent premium documentary look: cinematic lighting, believable textures, controlled
contrast/highlights, consistent grading, sharp/clean images. No visible generation
artifacts, no cursor icons/browser chrome/editing-interface overlays, no distorted text or
accidental logos. Apply the approved ORIVANTA visual identity consistently.

## 11. Editing — strictly prohibited
Frozen frames; reversed video loops; repeated footage as filler; unnecessary
forward-and-backward scene switching; random unrelated stock footage; long static images
replacing required video; unexplained black screens; poorly synced subtitles; mismatched
narration/visuals; unreadable graphics; repeated logos/unneeded intros; transitions that
interrupt storytelling. If suitable footage is unavailable, identify the gap and find the
best available solution — never silently substitute irrelevant material.

## 12. Resource management
Use existing subscriptions, generated footage, images, narration, music and SFX
intelligently; reuse good footage where editorially appropriate; don't regenerate
successful scenes without clear reason; prioritize available tools/free credits; no
purchasing credits/subscriptions without approval; if a generation limit is hit, use another
suitable available tool or schedule the remaining work across days. Never sacrifice quality
just to save a little time or resources.

## 13. Mandatory quality control
Review the complete film start to finish before delivery: every narration segment against
its visual, shot continuity/movement, absence of frozen frames/repeated loops, subtitle
accuracy/sync, correct maps/flags/names/dates/numbers, audio balance, consistent
grading/identity, opening hook and ending, final technical export. Never rely on a few
screenshots or isolated samples as proof of full-film quality. Provide a final QA report
listing verified results and any remaining limitations.

## 14. Permanent editorial principle
Every ORIVANTA documentary = accurate research + powerful storytelling + cinematic moving
visuals + precise synchronization + professional sound + meaningful information graphics.
The viewer should feel immersed in a premium international documentary, not watching an
AI-generated slideshow. Applies to every film, episode, trailer, short, and future
production. No publication, scheduling or public account changes without the owner's
explicit approval.

## Note on how this interacts with the delivered v4 film

`Haber_Bosch_Full_Film_v4.mp4` was built to fix the previous "back-and-forth looping"
complaint by replacing forward+reverse clip loops with: each real clip playing forward once,
then the remaining scene time filled with a Ken Burns (slow zoom/pan) move on that scene's
existing reference still.

**Rule 2 of this new standard directly prohibits that specific technique** — "never disguise
a still image with a slow zoom and present it as genuine video footage." So v4 fixed the
"back-and-forth" problem but not in a way that satisfies this permanent standard: large
portions of several scenes (most of scenes 2, 3, 5, 7, 8) are still-plus-zoom, not real
moving footage, which this standard now treats as a slideshow, not a documentary.

Bringing the film fully into line with this standard means generating enough real additional
video footage to cover each scene's full narration length (or re-timing narration/footage so
real clips carry the scene), which is new generation work with real credit/time cost — not
something to do silently. Flagging this now rather than either (a) quietly re-editing on the
assumption this standard overrides the "preserve resources" instruction, or (b) quietly
leaving v4 as the delivered film while calling it standard-compliant.
