Fritz Haber was a chemist at the technical university in Karlsruhe, Germany — ambitious, brilliant, and restless. He had begun studying the reaction that mattered: nitrogen plus hydrogen, making ammonia.

Haber had grown up in Breslau, then a German city, today Wrocław in Poland, in a Jewish family. He was proud, driven, and fiercely loyal to Germany.

In 1901, he married another chemist from Breslau: Clara Immerwahr.

Just months earlier, in December 1900, Clara had become the first woman to earn a doctorate from the University of Breslau. She defended her thesis in the university's main hall — and many young women of the city came to watch.

After the wedding, the expectations of the time left her little room for her own research. Her husband's career came first.

Remember her name. In Part 2, she becomes part of the story.

On paper, Haber's reaction is simple. In practice, it's a trap. Heat the gases, and the reaction speeds up — but the ammonia falls apart again. Cool them down, and almost nothing happens at all.

Haber's early measurements found only tiny traces of ammonia. And in May 1907, at a meeting of the Bunsen Society in Hamburg, Walther Nernst — one of the most respected chemists in Germany — publicly called Haber's numbers very inaccurate.

Haber was deeply offended. But Nernst had also proposed repeating the measurements at higher pressure. Haber took that idea further than anyone had dared.

To do it, he needed someone who could build equipment that didn't yet exist. That person was Robert Le Rossignol — a young British chemist from the island of Jersey, trained at University College London, who had joined Haber in Karlsruhe.

Le Rossignol built the high-pressure apparatus — with valves and seals that could hold gas at around two hundred times normal atmospheric pressure.

Near the end of his life, he summed up their partnership simply: Haber did the theoretical side, and he did the engineering side.

Their apparatus kept circulating the unreacted gas over a catalyst, drawing off ammonia as it formed. The rare metal osmium made it work.

By the summer of 1909, they were ready to show it to the people who could make it real.
