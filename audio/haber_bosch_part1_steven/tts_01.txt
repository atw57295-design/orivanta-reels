In 1898, the British scientist Sir William Crookes used his presidential address to the British Association to deliver a warning. The world, he said, was heading toward a wheat shortage. Populations were rising. Farmland was wearing out. And the thing crops needed most was running low.

That thing was nitrogen.

Plants can't use nitrogen gas. Its two atoms are bound so tightly that only a few things in nature can pull them apart — certain bacteria, and the violence of lightning. Everything else has to be recycled: manure, compost, the remains of living things.

By the nineteenth century, recycling wasn't enough. So the world went looking for nitrogen that was already fixed — and found it in two strange places.

The first was bird droppings: centuries of seabird guano, piled on islands off the coast of Peru and shipped to Europe by the ton.

The second was the Atacama Desert, one of the driest places on Earth, where vast beds of sodium nitrate lay just beneath the surface.

But in the 1870s, the richest of those deposits were not in Chile. They lay in Peru's southern province of Tarapacá, and along Bolivia's coast, around the port of Antofagasta. Much of the work there was done by Chilean and British companies.

In 1878, Bolivia tried to raise taxes on the Chilean-run Antofagasta Nitrate Company. When Bolivia threatened to seize the company's property, Chilean forces occupied Antofagasta on February fourteenth, eighteen seventy-nine. Bolivia declared war on Chile — and Chile went to war with both Bolivia and its ally, Peru.

It became known as the War of the Pacific. It lasted five years.

Chile won. In the Treaty of Ancón, in 1883, Peru gave up Tarapacá. A truce in 1884 gave Chile the entire Bolivian coast, with its nitrate, copper and mineral industries. A treaty in 1904 made it permanent.

Bolivia has had no coastline... ever since.

Chilean saltpeter became one of the world's most sought-after commodities.

And here's the problem. Nitrate didn't only grow wheat. It also made gunpowder and explosives. Whoever controlled those sea lanes controlled both food and firepower.

Crookes's message was simple: the deposits would not last. The way forward was to take nitrogen straight from the air.

Someone had to figure out how.
