Since 1908, Haber had been working under contract with BASF — the Badische Anilin- und Soda-Fabrik, one of the largest chemical companies in the world.

On July 2nd, 1909, Haber and Le Rossignol invited BASF to Karlsruhe for a demonstration. Carl Bosch was invited. But it was the chemist Alwin Mittasch and the company's chief mechanic, Julius Kranz, who watched.

What they saw was a machine that sat on a table.

And out of it, drop by drop, came liquid ammonia — made from the air. Roughly two cubic centimeters a minute, hour after hour.

It wasn't much. But it was continuous. It didn't stop.

Le Rossignol kept a sealed glass tube of the ammonia made that day. More than forty years later, he gave it to the Science Museum in London.

The next day, Haber sent BASF's directors a report of what they had witnessed.

But a machine on a table is not a factory. And there was a question that still hangs over this moment: whose invention was it?
